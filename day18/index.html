Day 18: Basic reporting queries on the warehouse

Thu 22 October 2026 · 15 queries · run on AdventureWorksDW2022

Goal: write the standard report shapes against a star schema and feel how much easier they are than on OLTP.

Pattern: FROM the fact, JOIN the dimensions you need on their keys, WHERE and GROUP BY on dimension attributes, aggregate the measures.

The pattern: every warehouse query is the same shape. FROM the fact, JOIN the dimensions you need on their keys, WHERE on dimension attributes, GROUP BY dimension attributes, aggregate the measures. Once you see it you cannot unsee it.
SELECT   <dimension attributes>, <AGG>(f.<measure>) AS <name>
FROM     <FactTable> f
JOIN     <DimTable> d1 ON f.<Dim1Key> = d1.<Dim1Key>
JOIN     <DimTable> d2 ON f.<Dim2Key> = d2.<Dim2Key>     -- one join per dimension used
WHERE    <dimension attribute> = <value>                  -- filter on dimensions
GROUP BY <the same dimension attributes>
ORDER BY <attribute or measure>;
Two warnings for this database: the warehouse's internet sales run from 29 December 2010 to 28 January 2014, so 2010 and 2014 are a few days and one month, not years (drills 1, 3 and 9). And a query that touches two fact tables must aggregate each one first (drill 6).

Drill

#1

1. Revenue by year and quarter

USE AdventureWorksDW2022;
SELECT d.CalendarYear, d.CalendarQuarter, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey
GROUP BY d.CalendarYear, d.CalendarQuarter ORDER BY 1, 2;

What this query does

The pattern in its simplest form: FROM the fact, JOIN DimDate on its key, GROUP BY two date attributes, SUM the measure. DimDate already holds CalendarYear and CalendarQuarter, so no DATEPART is needed. 14 quarters: the warehouse's sales start on 29 December 2010 and end on 28 January 2014, so 2010 Q4 (43,421) and 2014 Q1 (45,695) are only a few days or weeks of data. 2013 Q4 is the best quarter at 5.33 million.

📍 The first and last rows of any time series are often partial periods. Check the date range before comparing them.

#2

2. Revenue by product category

SELECT pc.EnglishProductCategoryName AS Category, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimProduct p ON f.ProductKey = p.ProductKey
JOIN DimProductSubcategory ps ON p.ProductSubcategoryKey = ps.ProductSubcategoryKey
JOIN DimProductCategory pc ON ps.ProductCategoryKey = pc.ProductCategoryKey
GROUP BY pc.EnglishProductCategoryName ORDER BY Revenue DESC;

What this query does

Bikes 28.32 million, Accessories 0.70 million, Clothing 0.34 million; together exactly the 29,358,677.22 total, so no sales were lost by the INNER JOINs. All three joins are on the dimension side: product to subcategory to category is a snowflake chain (Day 19). The fact table is read once.

📍 Bikes are 96% of internet revenue. Reconcile category totals with the fact total to prove the joins dropped nothing.

#3

3. Revenue by country and year, as a pivot

SELECT g.EnglishCountryRegionName AS Country,
       SUM(CASE WHEN d.CalendarYear = 2012 THEN f.SalesAmount ELSE 0 END) AS Y2012,
       SUM(CASE WHEN d.CalendarYear = 2013 THEN f.SalesAmount ELSE 0 END) AS Y2013,
       SUM(CASE WHEN d.CalendarYear = 2014 THEN f.SalesAmount ELSE 0 END) AS Y2014
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimCustomer c ON f.CustomerKey = c.CustomerKey
JOIN DimGeography g ON c.GeographyKey = g.GeographyKey
JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey
GROUP BY g.EnglishCountryRegionName ORDER BY Country;

What this query does

Six countries, one column per year built with conditional SUMs (Day 4). The United States leads 2013 with 5.46 million, Australia is second with 4.34 million. Country lives on DimGeography, reached through DimCustomer. The hard-coded columns miss 2010 and 2011 completely (7.1 million of revenue), and 2014 is only January. In SQL the columns are fixed when you write the query; a Power BI matrix creates one column per year it finds.

📍 A manual pivot only shows the years you typed. Add a total column or check the grand total so missing years are noticed.

#4

4. Customer demographics

SELECT c.Gender, c.MaritalStatus, c.EnglishEducation, COUNT(DISTINCT f.CustomerKey) AS Customers, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimCustomer c ON f.CustomerKey = c.CustomerKey
GROUP BY c.Gender, c.MaritalStatus, c.EnglishEducation ORDER BY Revenue DESC;

What this query does

20 groups (2 genders x 2 marital statuses x 5 education levels). Married men with a bachelor's degree are the top group: 1,634 customers, 2.76 million. COUNT(DISTINCT f.CustomerKey) counts customers, not order lines. In the OLTP database, gender, marital status and education are buried in an XML column (Person.Person.Demographics); the warehouse flattened them into plain columns at load time.

📍 One join for a demographic report. The hard work was done once, when DimCustomer was built.

#5

5. Weekday vs weekend sales

SELECT CASE WHEN d.DayNumberOfWeek IN (1, 7) THEN 'Weekend' ELSE 'Weekday' END AS DayType, COUNT(*) AS Lines, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey
GROUP BY CASE WHEN d.DayNumberOfWeek IN (1, 7) THEN 'Weekend' ELSE 'Weekday' END;

What this query does

In DimDate, DayNumberOfWeek 1 is Sunday and 7 is Saturday, so (1, 7) is the weekend. Weekdays: 43,320 lines and 21.12 million; weekend: 17,078 lines and 8.24 million. Per day that is 4.22 million per weekday and 4.12 million per weekend day: almost the same, so online buying does not depend on the day. The GROUP BY repeats the CASE because the alias is not visible to GROUP BY (Day 6).

📍 Compare per-day averages, not totals: there are five weekdays and two weekend days. Check what 1 and 7 mean in your date table before you trust it.

#6

6. Internet vs reseller: two facts, same dimension

SELECT 'Internet' AS Channel, d.CalendarYear, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey GROUP BY d.CalendarYear
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Reseller', d.CalendarYear, SUM(f.SalesAmount)
FROM FactResellerSales f JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey GROUP BY d.CalendarYear
ORDER BY CalendarYear, Channel;

What this query does

Each fact is aggregated to year first, then the two small results are stacked. Reseller sales are bigger every year (33.6 million vs 16.4 million in 2013), and resellers have no 2014 row. Joining the two facts directly on date would multiply every internet line by every reseller line on the same day. Because both facts use the same DimDate, "year" means the same thing on both sides: a conformed dimension (Day 19).

📍 Aggregate each fact to the shared grain, then combine. Never join two fact tables row to row.

#7

7. Margin by product

SELECT p.EnglishProductName, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue, SUM(f.TotalProductCost) AS Cost,
       SUM(f.SalesAmount - f.TotalProductCost) AS Margin,
       SUM(f.SalesAmount - f.TotalProductCost) / SUM(f.SalesAmount) * 100 AS MarginPct
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimProduct p ON f.ProductKey = p.ProductKey
GROUP BY p.EnglishProductName HAVING SUM(f.SalesAmount) > 100000 ORDER BY MarginPct DESC;

What this query does

53 products sold more than 100,000. Margin is computed per row (SalesAmount minus TotalProductCost), then summed: both are additive, so the difference is too. MarginPct is a ratio, so it is computed from the sums, never averaged from row percentages. Mountain-200 bikes earn the best margin (about 45.6%), the Road-150 about 39.3%. Grouping by name merges the history versions of a product (Day 16) into one row, which is what you want here.

📍 Additive measures can be combined row by row; ratios must be rebuilt from the totals.

#8

8. Role-playing dimension: DimDate three times

SELECT TOP 10 f.SalesOrderNumber, od.FullDateAlternateKey AS Ordered, sd.FullDateAlternateKey AS Shipped, dd.FullDateAlternateKey AS Due,
       DATEDIFF(day, od.FullDateAlternateKey, sd.FullDateAlternateKey) AS DaysToShip
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimDate od ON f.OrderDateKey = od.DateKey
JOIN DimDate sd ON f.ShipDateKey = sd.DateKey
JOIN DimDate dd ON f.DueDateKey = dd.DateKey;

What this query does

One DimDate table, joined three times with three aliases (od, sd, dd), plays three roles: order date, ship date and due date. Every order shown ships 7 days after it is ordered and is due 12 days after. TOP 10 without ORDER BY returns whichever rows SQL Server reads first, here the earliest orders.

📍 Same table, three aliases, three meanings. In Power BI only one relationship can be active, so you use USERELATIONSHIP or one date table per role.

#9

9. Year-over-year growth with LAG

WITH Yearly AS (
    SELECT d.CalendarYear, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
    FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey GROUP BY d.CalendarYear
)
SELECT CalendarYear, Revenue, LAG(Revenue) OVER (ORDER BY CalendarYear) AS PrevYear,
       (Revenue - LAG(Revenue) OVER (ORDER BY CalendarYear)) / LAG(Revenue) OVER (ORDER BY CalendarYear) * 100 AS GrowthPct
FROM Yearly;

What this query does

The CTE aggregates to one row per year, then LAG (Day 8) fetches the previous year's revenue. 2012 fell 17.4% and 2013 grew 179.9%. The 2011 figure (+16,195%) and the 2014 figure (-99.7%) are meaningless: 2010 has only 3 days of sales (29 to 31 December) and 2014 only 28 days. The SQL is right; the comparison is not.

📍 Before quoting growth, check that both periods are complete. Partial years make huge, false percentages.

#10

10a. Category revenue on the warehouse, with statistics

SET STATISTICS IO ON; SET STATISTICS TIME ON;
SELECT pc.EnglishProductCategoryName AS Category, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimProduct p ON f.ProductKey = p.ProductKey
JOIN DimProductSubcategory ps ON p.ProductSubcategoryKey = ps.ProductSubcategoryKey
JOIN DimProductCategory pc ON ps.ProductCategoryKey = pc.ProductCategoryKey
GROUP BY pc.EnglishProductCategoryName ORDER BY Revenue DESC;

What this query does

Drill 2 again with statistics on; the Messages tab shows the cost. FactInternetSales is one 1,249-page scan, DimProduct 253 pages (it is wide: descriptions in eight languages), the category tables a few pages. When I ran it, CPU time was 16 ms and elapsed time 27 ms.

📍 Three joins, no filter: everything the report needs is already in the fact and its dimensions.

#11

10b. The same question on the OLTP database

USE AdventureWorks2022;
SET STATISTICS IO ON; SET STATISTICS TIME ON;
SELECT pc.Name AS Category, SUM(d.LineTotal) AS Revenue
FROM Sales.SalesOrderDetail d
JOIN Production.Product p ON d.ProductID = p.ProductID
JOIN Production.ProductSubcategory ps ON p.ProductSubcategoryID = ps.ProductSubcategoryID
JOIN Production.ProductCategory pc ON ps.ProductCategoryID = pc.ProductCategoryID
JOIN Sales.SalesOrderHeader h ON d.SalesOrderID = h.SalesOrderID
WHERE h.OnlineOrderFlag = 1
GROUP BY pc.Name ORDER BY Revenue DESC;

What this query does

Same three totals. The OLTP version needs a fourth join, to SalesOrderHeader, only to filter online orders, because SalesOrderDetail mixes online and reseller lines (121,317 of them): 1,238 pages of detail plus 686 pages of header. When I ran it, CPU time was 110 ms and elapsed time 142 ms, roughly five times the warehouse query. On 120,000 rows both are fast; the gap grows with the data, and the warehouse query is also the easier one to write correctly.

📍 The warehouse wins twice: less work for the server and fewer joins (and filters) for the person writing the query.

Self-test

#12

S1. Revenue by month for Bikes in Australia

USE AdventureWorksDW2022;
SELECT d.CalendarYear, d.MonthNumberOfYear, d.EnglishMonthName, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey
JOIN DimProduct p ON f.ProductKey = p.ProductKey
JOIN DimProductSubcategory ps ON p.ProductSubcategoryKey = ps.ProductSubcategoryKey
JOIN DimProductCategory pc ON ps.ProductCategoryKey = pc.ProductCategoryKey
JOIN DimCustomer c ON f.CustomerKey = c.CustomerKey
JOIN DimGeography g ON c.GeographyKey = g.GeographyKey
WHERE pc.EnglishProductCategoryName = 'Bikes' AND g.EnglishCountryRegionName = 'Australia'
GROUP BY d.CalendarYear, d.MonthNumberOfYear, d.EnglishMonthName
ORDER BY d.CalendarYear, d.MonthNumberOfYear;

What this query does

37 months, December 2010 to December 2013 (Australia bought no bikes online in January 2014). The template filled in: WHERE on two dimension attributes (category, country), GROUP BY three date attributes, SUM one measure. MonthNumberOfYear is grouped and sorted so January comes before February; sorting by EnglishMonthName would put April first.

📍 Six joins, but every one is fact to dimension or dimension to dimension. The pattern does not change as the question grows.

#13

S2. Distinct customers per education level

SELECT c.EnglishEducation, COUNT(DISTINCT f.CustomerKey) AS Customers
FROM FactInternetSales f JOIN DimCustomer c ON f.CustomerKey = c.CustomerKey
GROUP BY c.EnglishEducation ORDER BY Customers DESC;

What this query does

Bachelors 5,356, Partial College 5,064, High School 3,294, Graduate Degree 3,189, Partial High School 1,581: 18,484 in total, every customer exactly once. COUNT(*) would count order lines (60,398) instead of people.

📍 "How many customers" needs COUNT(DISTINCT key) on a fact; COUNT(*) counts rows at the fact's grain.

#14

S3. Average days to ship per year

SELECT d.CalendarYear, AVG(CAST(DATEDIFF(day, od.FullDateAlternateKey, sd.FullDateAlternateKey) AS DECIMAL(5,2))) AS AvgDaysToShip, COUNT(*) AS Lines
FROM FactInternetSales f
JOIN DimDate od ON f.OrderDateKey = od.DateKey
JOIN DimDate sd ON f.ShipDateKey = sd.DateKey
JOIN DimDate d ON f.OrderDateKey = d.DateKey
GROUP BY d.CalendarYear ORDER BY d.CalendarYear;

What this query does

Exactly 7.00 days in every year, because every internet order in the sample ships 7 days after it is ordered (the minimum and maximum are both 7). The order-date role is joined twice here, once for the date value and once for the year; od.CalendarYear would do the same job with one join less.

📍 Sample data is often too regular. A real business would show a spread here, and you would report it with a median or a histogram too.

#15

S4. Which reseller business type buys the most

SELECT r.BusinessType, COUNT(DISTINCT f.ResellerKey) AS Resellers, SUM(f.SalesAmount) AS Revenue,
       SUM(f.SalesAmount) / COUNT(DISTINCT f.ResellerKey) AS RevenuePerReseller
FROM FactResellerSales f JOIN DimReseller r ON f.ResellerKey = r.ResellerKey
GROUP BY r.BusinessType ORDER BY Revenue DESC;

What this query does

Warehouses buy the most: 38.73 million from 219 resellers (176,835 each). Value Added Resellers are close behind at 34.97 million. Specialty Bike Shops are almost as many (200) but buy only 6.76 million, 33,781 each. "Buys the most" can mean the total or the average per reseller; the query shows both.

📍 When a question says "most", show the total and the per-member average: they can rank differently.

Self-test: answers

The pattern from memory: see the template at the top of the page: SELECT dimension attributes and aggregated measures, FROM the fact, JOIN each dimension on its key, WHERE on dimension attributes, GROUP BY the same attributes.
Revenue by month for Bikes in Australia (query S1): six joins, WHERE category = Bikes AND country = Australia, GROUP BY year and month number. 37 months from December 2010 to December 2013.

Distinct customers per education level (S2): COUNT(DISTINCT f.CustomerKey) grouped by EnglishEducation. Bachelors 5,356, Partial College 5,064, High School 3,294, Graduate Degree 3,189, Partial High School 1,581; together all 18,484 customers.

Average days to ship per year (S3): DimDate joined as order date and as ship date, AVG of DATEDIFF. Exactly 7.00 in every year: every internet order in the sample ships in 7 days.

Which reseller business type buys the most (S4): Warehouse, 38.73 million from 219 resellers (176,835 each), ahead of Value Added Reseller (34.97 million) and Specialty Bike Shop (6.76 million, 33,781 each).
